Financial Literacy Hub

WDD 131 — Site Plan Document

Section 1

Site Name

Financial Literacy Hub — A name that's both descriptive and aspirational. "Financial Literacy" tells visitors exactly what they're getting, and "Hub" conveys that this is a central place to come back to — not just a one-off article page, but an ongoing resource.

The name was chosen because it communicates purpose immediately without jargon. Young people who feel intimidated by finance-related topics will still feel welcome — the word "Hub" is approachable and familiar. The name also scales well whether the site grows to cover investing, budgeting, credit, or taxes.

Optional domain: finlithub.org
Section 2

Site Purpose

The Financial Literacy Hub is designed for young adults — particularly students and recent graduates — who want to take control of their money but don't know where to start. The site covers the fundamentals: budgeting, saving, understanding credit, and early investing concepts.

Beyond articles, the site will provide practical tools like a simple budget calculator, a glossary of financial terms, and a curated list of trusted external resources. A contact/newsletter signup form will help build community around the content and keep returning visitors updated when new material is added.

Section 3

Scenarios

These are questions a real visitor might type into a search engine and end up on our site. Thinking about them upfront makes sure the content we create actually answers people's real problems — not just what we think they want.

  • "I just got my first paycheck — how do I make a budget that actually sticks and doesn't feel impossible to follow?"
  • "What does a credit score actually mean, and how can I start building good credit while I'm still in school?"
  • "I've saved a small amount of money — is it too early to start investing, and if not, where do I even begin?"
Section 4

Color Schema

The palette was built around trust and clarity — the two things a financial site needs most. Deep navy grounds the design with authority and seriousness, while golden amber brings warmth and optimism without losing professionalism. The off-white surface keeps things calm and readable for longer sessions.

Section 5

Typography

Two fonts are used — enough variety to create visual hierarchy without the mess of juggling three competing typefaces. Both are loaded from Google Fonts for speed and broad device support.

Resource used: Choosing Web Fonts: A Beginner's Guide — Google Design

Font 1 — Headings
Playfair Display

Used for: all headings (h1–h3), site name, section titles
Weight: 600 & 700 · Style: Serif
Why: Playfair Display has a classic editorial feel — it signals credibility and expertise, which is exactly what a financial resource needs. The serifs add a slight formality that body fonts don't need to carry.

Font 2 — Body
DM Sans — "Knowing where your money goes is the first step to making it work for you."

Used for: paragraphs, navigation, labels, captions, buttons
Weight: 300, 400 & 500 · Style: Sans-serif
Why: DM Sans is clean without being sterile. It pairs well with Playfair Display because the contrast between serif and sans-serif creates clear visual hierarchy while keeping the page easy to read at length.
